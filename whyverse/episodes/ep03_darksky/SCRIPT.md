## Cold open (0:01)

- 0:01 · Go outside tonight and look up. Between the stars, the sky is black.
- 0:06 · Obvious, right? The Sun has set, so it's dark.
- 0:09 · But for about four hundred years, that darkness was one of the great puzzles of astronomy.
- 0:15 · Because if the universe were endless, eternal, and full of stars, the night sky shouldn't be dark at all. It should blaze, in every direction, as bright as the surface of a star.
- 0:27 · The real answer turns out to be one of the best clues we have about where everything came from.
- 0:32 · So, why is the sky dark at night?

## Chapter 1 — A forest of stars (0:40)

- 0:40 · Chapter one. A forest of stars.
- 0:43 · **HOOK:** Here's the trick question: if the stars go on forever, where could you look and not see one?
- 0:49 · Imagine you're standing in the middle of a huge forest.
- 0:52 · Look in any direction, and your view ends on a tree trunk. Near trees look wide, far trees look thin. But if the forest is big enough, there are no gaps. Only wood.
- 1:03 · Now swap the trees for stars. If space went on forever, evenly filled with stars, every line of sight would end on the surface of a star.
- 1:12 · No black gaps. Just one continuous wall of starlight, day and night, as bright as the face of the Sun.
- 1:20 · Johannes Kepler worried about this in sixteen ten. Edmond Halley took it on in the seventeen twenties.
- 1:26 · But the puzzle is named after a German doctor and astronomer, Heinrich Olbers, who described it in eighteen twenty three. Olbers' paradox.

## Chapter 2 — Far away doesn't help (1:36)

- 1:36 · Chapter two. Far away doesn't help.
- 1:39 · **HOOK:** The obvious answer is that distant stars are just too faint to matter. Surprisingly, that doesn't work.
- 1:46 · Picture the stars arranged in shells around us, like the layers of an onion, every shell the same thickness.
- 1:53 · Take a shell twice as far away. Each star in it looks four times fainter.
- 1:58 · But that shell is bigger. It holds four times as many stars.
- 2:02 · Four times fainter, four times as many. They cancel out exactly. So every shell sends us the same amount of light.
- 2:10 · Add up shell after shell, forever, and the light just keeps piling up. Far away stars don't fade into nothing. They fill in the gaps.
- 2:19 · Do the sum properly, and the result is shocking. The whole sky should shine like the surface of the Sun, at thousands of degrees. Instead of a night, we'd get an oven.

## Chapter 3 — The wrong answers (2:30)

- 2:30 · Chapter three. The wrong answers.
- 2:33 · **HOOK:** So could something out there be blocking the light? It's a clever idea. It just doesn't work.
- 2:39 · Olbers himself thought that clouds of dust and gas might soak up the distant starlight.
- 2:44 · But anything that absorbs light warms up. Give it enough time, and the dust gets as hot as the starlight around it, and starts to glow just as brightly.
- 2:54 · The blocker becomes part of the wall of light.
- 2:57 · What about stars clumping into galaxies, with empty space in between? That only rearranges the forest. If it goes on forever, the gaps still close.
- 3:07 · Something more basic had to be wrong with the picture.

## Chapter 4 — A poet's answer (3:12)

- 3:12 · Chapter four. A poet's answer.
- 3:15 · **HOOK:** The first person to guess the right answer wasn't an astronomer. He was a poet.
- 3:20 · In eighteen forty eight, Edgar Allan Poe, the writer of The Raven, published a long, strange essay about the universe, called Eureka.
- 3:29 · In it, he suggested the background could be so far away that its light simply hasn't reached us yet.
- 3:35 · Light is fast, but not infinitely fast. And the universe hasn't been around forever.
- 3:40 · When you look at the Moon, you see it as it was about a second ago. The Sun, as it was eight minutes ago. The Andromeda galaxy, two and a half million years ago.
- 3:51 · It's about thirteen point eight billion years old. So we can only see light that has had time to get here. Everything farther out is still on its way.
- 4:00 · Inside that bubble there are an enormous number of stars. But not nearly enough to cover the whole sky. Between them, there are gaps.
- 4:08 · Our telescopes can see deep into that bubble. Webb has spotted galaxies whose light left them less than three hundred million years after the Big Bang.
- 4:18 · In nineteen oh one, Lord Kelvin did the math and showed this really works. To fill every gap, light would need around ten to the power of twenty three years. The universe is trillions of times too young. And stars burn out long before that anyway.

## Chapter 5 — The glowing wall (4:35)

- 4:35 · Chapter five. The glowing wall.
- 4:38 · **HOOK:** Here's the twist: in a way, Olbers was right. There really is a glowing wall behind the stars.
- 4:44 · Space is expanding. As light crosses it, its waves get stretched, so the light gets redder and weaker. That makes distant galaxies even dimmer.
- 4:54 · It's one reason the James Webb Space Telescope looks in infrared. The light of the first galaxies has been stretched right out of the colours our eyes can see.
- 5:04 · Now run the clock backwards. Early on, the whole universe was filled with hot, glowing gas, at about three thousand degrees. Like the surface of a cool star.
- 5:14 · Back then, the sky really was bright in every direction. Olbers' wall of light was real.
- 5:20 · About three hundred and eighty thousand years after the Big Bang, the gas cooled enough for light to fly free. That light is still arriving, from every direction.
- 5:30 · But by now it has been stretched about a thousand times. It has cooled to under three degrees above absolute zero. It isn't visible light anymore. It's microwaves.
- 5:41 · In nineteen sixty four, two scientists at Bell Labs, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, heard a strange hiss in their antenna that came from everywhere. They even cleaned out pigeon droppings to get rid of it. The hiss stayed. It was this glow.
- 5:56 · If your eyes could see microwaves, the night sky wouldn't be dark at all. It would glow, almost perfectly evenly, everywhere you look.

## Chapter 6 — The darkness we're losing (6:06)

- 6:06 · Chapter six. The darkness we're losing.
- 6:09 · **HOOK:** So the dark sky is a clue to how the universe began. And most of us can't really see it anymore.
- 6:16 · In twenty sixteen, a world atlas of light pollution found that more than a third of humanity can no longer see the Milky Way. That includes nearly eighty percent of North Americans.
- 6:27 · From a truly dark place, you can see around two and a half thousand stars at once. From the middle of a big city, often only a few dozen.
- 6:36 · And it's getting worse. More than fifty thousand people counting stars by eye showed that the night sky got about ten percent brighter every year, from twenty eleven to twenty twenty two.
- 6:48 · At that rate, a child born where two hundred and fifty stars are visible would see only about a hundred by their eighteenth birthday.
- 6:56 · And it's not only about the view. Bright lights at night confuse migrating birds, and lead newly hatched sea turtles away from the sea.
- 7:05 · The fixes are simple. Shielded lights that point down, warmer bulbs, and switching off what nobody needs.
