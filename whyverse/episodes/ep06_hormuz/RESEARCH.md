## Geography
- Narrowest width ≈ 33 km (21 miles; Reuters factbox 4 Feb 2026, EIA via Scientific American); some sources 21 nautical miles ≈ 39 km → said "33 to 39 km" in c1, "about 33 km" in the cold open. [2]

- Marathon = 42.195 km (World Athletics standard) → the gap is narrower than a marathon.
- Length ≈ 167 km (90 nmi). [1, Wikipedia "Strait of Hormuz"]
- Shipping lanes: each direction 2 miles (≈ 3 km) wide with a 2-mile buffer (Traffic Separation Scheme off Musandam). [2] Reuters factbox; EIA/SciAm. On screen the lanes are drawn wider than real ("lanes drawn wider than real, to be visible").
- Loaded supertanker draft 20–25 m; water under 25 m unsafe for a fully laden VLCC. [1] Straus Center https://www.strausscenter.org/?p=1943 → "as deep as a seven-storey building is tall" (≈ 3 m per storey; illustration).
- Borders: Iran (north), Oman's Musandam exclave (south). [2] Britannica; Wikipedia.

## Flows
- ~20 million barrels a day of crude + products in 2025; ~25 % of seaborne oil trade; Qatar ~93 % and UAE ~96 % of LNG exports via the strait = 19 % of global LNG; "There are no alternative routes to bring these volumes to market". [1, IEA primary]
  IEA Strait of Hormuz factsheet, Feb 2026: https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/c8248eba-8689-46d9-ae4b-b858b59c0f1c/StraitofHormuz2026-Factsheet.pdf
- EIA: 20.9 mb/d in H1 2025 (21.8 in 2023, 20.7 in 2024) ≈ 20 % of global petroleum liquids consumption, ≈ a quarter of seaborne oil trade; >20 % of LNG; 84 % of the crude and 83 % of the LNG went to Asia in 2024; China, India, Japan, South Korea = 69 %. [2 with IEA]
  https://www.eia.gov/international/content/analysis/special_topics/World_Oil_Transit_Chokepoints · https://eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=65504
- "One in five barrels the world burned" = EIA's ~20 % of global consumption (pre-war).
- VLCC ≈ 2 million barrels, ≈ 330 m long (Britannica "very large crude carrier"); 330 m ≈ 3 football pitches (≈ 105 m each). Al Jazeera 12 Nov: two supertankers carried "more than four million barrels between them".
- Math (ours, shown on screen): 20,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 231 barrels/s; 20 M × 0.159 m³ = 3.18 M m³/day ÷ 2,500 m³ (Olympic pool) ≈ 1,270 pools/day ≈ one every 68 s ("roughly every minute"); 20 M ÷ 2 M = 10 VLCCs a day.

## Back doors
- Saudi East–West pipeline (Abqaiq → Yanbu, Red Sea): design 5 mb/d (Aramco reported 7 mb/d in 2025, untested), ~1,200 km (746 miles, EIA). UAE ADCOP Habshan → Fujairah ≈ 1.5–1.8 mb/d. Iran Goreh → Jask: "effectively non-operational" (IEA). Total spare capacity 3.5–5.5 mb/d (IEA factsheet 2026). [2: IEA + EIA (EIA: ~2.6 mb/d unused, 2025)]

## The Tanker War (1984–88)
- 451 ships hit (61 % tankers), more than 400 civilian sailors killed, ships from ≥ 38 nations. [1] USIP Iran Primer https://iranprimer.usip.org/node/6146
- Both Iran and Iraq attacked shipping to hit each other's oil revenue. [2] USIP; Reuters factbox.
- 24 Jul 1987 tanker Bridgeton struck a mine; Operation Earnest Will (US escort of reflagged Kuwaiti tankers, Jul 1987–Sep 1988). [2] NDU https://ndupress.ndu.edu/Media/News/News-Article-View/Article/3106465/ ; Wikipedia "Operation Earnest Will"
- "Even at its most intense point, the Tanker War failed to disrupt more than two percent of ships passing through the Persian Gulf"; "Iran lowered the price of oil to offset higher insurance premiums"; "the real global oil price steadily declined during the 1980s"; initially "a sharp rise in the price of crude oil". [1] Strauss Center https://www.strausscenter.org/strait-of-hormuz-tanker-war/
- 1986: prices fell from ~$27 to below $10; Brent dipped under $9 in July 1986; cause: glut and Saudi/OPEC price war, not the attacks. [2] Wikipedia "1980s oil glut" + "1980–1989 world oil market chronology".
- Brent yearly averages on the chart (Energy Institute Statistical Review, nominal $): 1980 36.83 · 81 35.93 · 82 32.97 · 83 29.55 · 84 28.78 · 85 27.56 · 86 14.43 · 87 18.44 · 88 14.92. The "short spikes" marks are schematic (not data).
