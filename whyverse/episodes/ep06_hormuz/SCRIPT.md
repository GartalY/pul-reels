# Ep06 — Why can one narrow strait shake the world? (Strait of Hormuz) — SCRIPT

Voice: Kokoro af_heart, en-us. One line = one wav (vo/<id>.wav). Numbers spelled out for the voice.


## c0

- **c0_1** — Somewhere off the coast of Oman, a ship as long as three football fields is waiting. In its belly: two million barrels of oil.
- **c0_2** — Ahead of it lies a gap of water only about thirty-three kilometres wide. Narrower than a marathon.
- **c0_3** — Yet before this year, about one in every five barrels of oil the world burned passed through this one gap.
- **c0_4** — And in twenty twenty-six, when the ships almost stopped coming, oil prices around the world shot up by more than half.
- **c0_5** — By the end of this video, you'll know why one narrow strait can shake the whole world. And why, forty years ago, it didn't.
- **c0_6** — So, what makes the Strait of Hormuz so powerful?

## c1

- **c1_1** — Chapter one. A door in the map.
- **c1_1h** — Look closely at the Persian Gulf. Do you notice something strange?
- **c1_2** — It is almost a lake. A long, shallow sea, with Iran on one side and the Arabian Peninsula on the other.
- **c1_3** — There is only one way out to the open ocean. Right here, where the tip of Oman's Musandam peninsula reaches toward Iran.
- **c1_4** — That doorway is the Strait of Hormuz. About a hundred and sixty-seven kilometres long, and at its narrowest, only thirty-three to thirty-nine kilometres across.
- **c1_5** — But giant tankers can't use all of that. They follow two marked lanes, each only about three kilometres wide. One lane in, one lane out, with a buffer between them.
- **c1_6** — A fully loaded supertanker sits as deep in the water as a seven-storey building is tall, so it has to stay in the deep channel.
- **c1_7** — That's it. Two lanes, three kilometres each. The world's busiest oil doorway.
- **c1_8** — But why does so much oil need to pass through here in the first place?

## c2

- **c2_1** — Chapter two. A fifth of the world.
- **c2_1h** — How much is actually moving through this door?
- **c2_2** — Behind it sit some of the biggest oil producers on Earth. Saudi Arabia, Iraq, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, Iran. And Qatar, one of the world's largest exporters of natural gas.
- **c2_3** — In twenty twenty-five, about twenty million barrels of oil and fuel passed through the strait. Every single day.
- **c2_3b** — That's like ten fully loaded supertankers, like the one we met, leaving every day.
- **c2_4** — That is around two hundred and thirty barrels every second. Enough to fill an Olympic swimming pool roughly every minute.
- **c2_5** — It was about a fifth of all the oil the world uses, and a quarter of all the oil traded by sea.
- **c2_6** — Plus about a fifth of the world's liquefied natural gas.
- **c2_7** — Most of it heads east. More than eighty percent of the crude goes to Asia, mainly to China, India, Japan and South Korea.
- **c2_8** — So one doorway feeds a huge part of the world's engines. Surely there's a back door?

## c3

- **c3_1** — Chapter three. The back doors.
- **c3_1h** — If the front door is so risky, why not just go around it?
- **c3_2** — Some countries tried. Saudi Arabia built a pipeline right across the desert, about twelve hundred kilometres, to the Red Sea.
- **c3_3** — The United Arab Emirates built one to the port of Fujairah, outside the strait. Iran built one too, to Jask, but it has barely been used.
- **c3_4** — Add up the spare room in these pipes, and the International Energy Agency estimates roughly three and a half to five and a half million barrels a day.
- **c3_5** — Against twenty million. At best, about one barrel in four could take the back door.
- **c3_6** — And for gas, there is no back door at all. Almost all of Qatar's gas exports leave through the strait, with no other route to market.
- **c3_7** — So the world depends on this door staying open. Has anyone tried to close it before?

## c4

- **c4_1** — Chapter four. The Tanker War.
- **c4_1h** — Here is something many people believe: if the shooting starts, the oil stops.
- **c4_2** — In the nineteen eighties, Iran and Iraq were at war. From nineteen eighty-four, both sides attacked ships in the Gulf, to hit each other's oil money.
- **c4_3** — Over the next four years, four hundred and fifty-one ships were hit. More than four hundred sailors lost their lives.
- **c4_4** — In nineteen eighty-seven, the tanker Bridgeton struck a mine. The United States began escorting Kuwaiti tankers in convoys.
- **c4_5** — It sounds like the oil must have stopped. It didn't.
- **c4_6** — Even at the worst moments, the attacks hit no more than about two percent of the ships passing through. Iran even cut its oil prices to make up for the higher insurance.
- **c4_7** — There were short price spikes. But across the decade, prices kept sliding. And in nineteen eighty-six, for reasons that had nothing to do with the war, they crashed below ten dollars a barrel.
- **c4_8** — So attacks alone didn't close the door. What would it take?

## c5

- **c5_1** — Chapter five. The year the door almost shut.
- **c5_1h** — In twenty twenty-six, something happened that never happened in the Tanker War. The ships themselves stopped coming.
- **c5_2** — On February the twenty-eighth, the United States and Israel began strikes on Iran. Within days, Iran declared the strait closed.
- **c5_3** — Then came the part most people never see. Insurers pulled their war-risk cover. And without insurance, most ship owners simply won't sail.
- **c5_3b** — War-risk premiums jumped from a fraction of a percent of a ship's value to several percent, for a single voyage.
- **c5_4** — Before the war, more than a hundred ships passed through on a normal day. By the summer, it was around five a day. A drop of about ninety-five percent.
- **c5_5** — Brent crude, the world's benchmark oil price, had been around seventy dollars a barrel. At the end of April, it briefly touched about a hundred and twenty-six.
- **c5_6** — Governments opened their emergency tanks. In March, the thirty-two members of the International Energy Agency agreed to release a record four hundred million barrels.
- **c5_7** — That sounds enormous. But it equals only about twenty days of what normally flows through Hormuz.
- **c5_8** — By the autumn, ships were moving again, but only a fraction of the old traffic. This week, Iran said several tankers hit mines in the strait, a claim that could not be independently verified. Talks have stalled.
- **c5_9** — But wait. If only part of the oil went missing, why did prices jump so much?

## c6

- **c6_1** — Chapter six. Small gap, big price.
- **c6_1h** — You don't need to lose much oil for prices to explode. Here's why.
- **c6_2** — In nineteen seventy-nine, during the Iranian revolution, world oil supply fell by only about five percent. Prices more than doubled.
- **c6_3** — The reason is simple. In the short run, almost nobody can stop using oil. Trucks still deliver food. Planes still fly. Farms still need diesel.
- **c6_4** — When buyers can't cut back, even a small shortage turns into a bidding war. The price climbs until enough people are priced out.
- **c6_5** — And the cost spreads. In the United States, crude oil makes up about half of what drivers pay at the pump.
- **c6_6** — Then it rides along into everything that moves on wheels, wings or keels. Which is almost everything.
- **c6_7** — A narrow door, plus a fuel nobody can quickly live without. That's a shock that reaches your wallet.

## c7

- **c7_1** — So, why can one narrow strait shake the whole world?
- **c7_2** — Because geography gave the Gulf just one door. Because a fifth of the world's oil, and a fifth of its gas, go through it. Because the back doors are small. And because, in the short run, the world can't stop using oil.
- **c7_3** — Forty years ago, attacks alone could not shut that door. Ships kept sailing. This year, risk itself did it. No insurance, no ships.
- **c7_4** — That supertanker from the beginning is waiting for one thing: the moment the door feels safe again. And until then, the whole world pays for every barrel that stays behind.
