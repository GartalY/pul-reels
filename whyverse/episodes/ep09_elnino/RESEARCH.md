# EP09 — El Niño: fact sheet (verified)

Researched Sat 21 Nov 2026. Every figure below was read on the source page during research (WebFetch), unless marked **DERIVED** (simple arithmetic from sourced numbers) or **NOT VERIFIED**.
Format: claim (spoken English) · figure · source 1 · source 2 · avoid / careful wording.

Key: C3S = Copernicus Climate Change Service; CPC = NOAA Climate Prediction Center; WMO = World Meteorological Organization.

## A. NOW (late 2026)

**1. The Niño 3.4 patch of the Pacific hit its hottest temperature ever measured in October 2026.**
- Figure: monthly mean SST 29.94 °C in October 2026, an all-time high for the region.
- Source 1: C3S, "Surface air temperature for October 2026" bulletin (Nov 2026) — https://climate.copernicus.eu/surface-air-temperature-october-2026
- Source 2: C3S press release, 5–6 Nov 2026 — https://climate.copernicus.eu/copernicus-warmest-october-record-2026-virtually-certain-exceed-15degc ; also C3S on X — https://x.com/CopernicusECMWF/status/2120186544950788450
- Careful: this is a monthly mean for the whole box, in the ERA5 dataset. Say "hottest month on record for that patch", not "the hottest the ocean has ever been". Live Science gives the same figure as "above 86 °F" — https://www.livescience.com/planet-earth/rivers-oceans/the-central-pacific-ocean-just-broke-a-temperature-record-experts-say-it-might-signal-a-new-kind-of-el-nino

**2. That patch was 3.21 °C warmer than normal, also a record.**
- Figure: +3.21 °C above the 1991–2020 average, beating September's record of +2.99 °C.
- Source 1: C3S October 2026 bulletin (as #1).
- Source 2: C3S press release, 5 Nov 2026 (as #1).
- Careful: the baseline is 1991–2020, not pre-industrial. Don't mix it with the 1.75 °C air-temperature figure.

**3. Both numbers beat the peaks of the three great El Niños of 1982–83, 1997–98 and 2015–16.**
- Figure: the qualitative comparison only. C3S does not give the old peak values on that page.
- Source 1: C3S press release, 5 Nov 2026 (as #1). Source 2: C3S on X (as #1).
- Careful: say "beat the peaks of", not "X degrees hotter than". The old peak numbers depend on which index you use (see #15).

**4. October 2026 was the warmest October ever recorded for the planet's air.**
- Figure: 15.35 °C global mean surface air temperature; +0.90 °C vs 1991–2020; **1.75 °C above pre-industrial (1850–1900)**. Third month in a row above 1.5 °C. Previous October record: 2023 (+0.85 °C).
- Source 1: C3S October 2026 bulletin (as #1).
- Source 2: Down To Earth — https://www.downtoearth.org.in/climate-change/world-records-hottest-october-in-2026-global-temperatures-rise-175c-above-pre-industrial-levels ; Nation of Change, 10 Nov 2026 — https://www.nationofchange.org/2026/11/10/october-2026-was-the-warmest-october-on-record-copernicus-says/
- Careful: one month above 1.5 °C does not mean "the Paris limit is broken". Paris refers to long-term (20-year) averages.

**5. The global ocean surface also set a record in October 2026.**
- Figure: 20.95 °C average SST (60°S–60°N), highest October on record and the fifth monthly record in a row.
- Source 1: C3S October 2026 bulletin. Source 2: C3S press release, 5 Nov 2026.

**6. Scientists' framing: El Niño is adding heat on top of human warming.**
- Quote: "An exceptionally strong El Niño is adding further heat to a climate already warmed by human activities." Samantha Burgess, ECMWF/C3S, 5 Nov 2026.
- Source 1: C3S press release (as #1).
- Second voice: WMO's Celeste Saulo, Oct 2026: "El Niño doesn't add new heat to the planet." It redistributes heat stored in the ocean. https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/sea-surface-temperatures-reflect-strengthening-el-nino
- Careful: both statements are true and fit together. El Niño moves heat out of the ocean into the air; it does not create heat.

**7. 2026 will almost certainly finish more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial, probably as the second-hottest year.**
- Figure: >99% chance of exceeding 1.5 °C; predicted 1.56–1.61 °C; ~80% chance of being second-warmest, ~20% chance of tying or beating 2024.
- Source 1: C3S press release, 5 Nov 2026 (as #1).
- Source 2: Met Office blog, 21 Aug 2026 (Nick Dunstone): "2027 is very likely to replace 2024 as the warmest year on record." https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2026/an-unprecedented-el-nio-and-its-implications-for-the-weather-forecast
- Careful: **do not say 2026 is the hottest year.** The biggest global temperature boost usually comes in the year after an El Niño peaks, so the record is expected in 2027. Keep that forecast as "very likely".

**8. NOAA's official status in November 2026: an El Niño Advisory, and it is still strengthening.**
- Figure: CPC ENSO Diagnostic Discussion, 12 Nov 2026. Niño-3.4 at +2.5 °C, Niño-3 at +3.6 °C, Niño-1+2 at +4.4 °C. Surface anomalies in the eastern equatorial Pacific exceeded 4.0 °C. Subsurface anomalies exceeded +10 °C at depth. Indonesia remains dry.
- Source 1: https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/ensodisc.shtml (next update 10 Dec 2026)
- Source 2: WMO, 8 Oct 2026 — https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/sea-surface-temperatures-reflect-strengthening-el-nino
- Careful: NOAA's +2.5 °C and C3S's +3.21 °C differ because the datasets and baselines differ. NOAA now uses a "relative" index (see #16). Pick one and name it on screen.

**9. NOAA gives about 60% odds that this becomes the strongest El Niño since 1950, a "historic" event.**
- Figure: ~60% chance for Oct–Dec and Nov–Jan of a 3-month RONI ≥ +2.5 °C, which would exceed any El Niño since 1950. >79% chance of strong-to-very-strong El Niño through Jan–Mar 2027.
- Source 1: CPC discussion, 12 Nov 2026 (as #8).
- Source 2: Gizmodo, "NOAA raises odds of a historic super El Niño" — https://gizmodo.com/noaa-raises-odds-of-a-historic-super-el-nino-2000784065 (not opened; seen in search results only)
- Careful: say "about a 60% chance", not "will be".

**10. It is expected to peak around December and last into early 2027.**
- Figure: WMO says there is a nearly 100% chance it persists through February 2027 (consensus of 13 forecasting centres). WMO forecasts an Oct–Dec Niño 3.4 anomaly of about +3.7 °C, peaking around December; the previous record was +2.6 °C (Nov 2015–Jan 2016). CPC expects it to strengthen further, then weaken in early 2027.
- Source 1: WMO, 8 Oct 2026 (as #8).
- Source 2: CGTN, 9 Oct 2026 — https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-10-09/WMO-El-Ni-o-forecast-to-peak-in-December-last-through-February-2027-1R6t7WLkY48/p.html ; Euronews, updated 20 Nov 2026 ("isn't expected to end until spring 2027") — https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/20/when-will-el-nino-peak-and-end-a-full-timeline-of-the-weather-phenomenon-taking-the-world-
- Careful: +3.7 °C is a forecast. C3S (#2) uses a different baseline.

**11. "El Niño is being supersized before our eyes."** UN Secretary-General António Guterres, quoted by WMO.
- Source 1: WMO, 3 Sep 2026 — https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/el-nino-set-become-very-strong-raising-risks-of-extreme-weather-2027
- Also from that release: Celeste Saulo, "This exceptional El Niño demands exceptional preparation and response."

**12. NOAA's US winter outlook for 2026–27: warmer north, wetter south.**
- Figure: Dec 2026–Feb 2027. Wetter than average across the southern tier from the Southwest to the Southeast and along the East Coast. Warmer than average in northern California, the Pacific Northwest, the Midwest, much of the East and Alaska. Drought expected to improve in the Southwest and Southeast. NWS director Ken Graham: "a very strong El Nino will likely bring a southward and eastward shift to the jet stream."
- Source 1: NOAA news release, 15 Oct 2026 — https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/el-nino-looms-large-over-us-winter-outlook
- Source 2: ABC News — https://abcnews.com/US/noaa-forecasts-wet-south-warmer-north-el-nino/story?id=137288754
- Careful: these are tilted odds, not guarantees. Say "favours" or "tilts the odds".

## B. HISTORY & NAME

**14. The name: Peruvian fishermen called a warm current that arrived around Christmas "El Niño", the (Christ) child.**
- Source 1: Britannica, "El Niño". The name was "first used in the 19th century" by northern Peruvian fishermen for the annual warm flow "around Christmas time". https://www.britannica.com/science/El-Nino
- Source 2: NOAA Ocean Explorer — https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/elnino.html ; NOAA PMEL ("The Little Boy" or "Christ child") — https://pmel.noaa.gov/elnino/node/2
- Careful: NOAA's "Understanding El Niño" page says fishermen recognised it "in the 1600s", but other sources say the 1800s. Safest: "Peruvian fishermen noticed it centuries ago". The word first meant a yearly Christmas-time current; only later did it mean the big irregular events.

**15. One of the first written records: in 1892 the Peruvian navy captain Camilo Carrillo told a geographical society congress in Lima that sailors called the warm current "El Niño".**
- Source 1: Mental Floss, "14 Fascinating Facts about El Niño" — https://www.mentalfloss.com/science/weather/14-fascinating-facts-about-el-nino
- Source 2: Religion Unplugged, 14 Oct 2026 — https://religionunplugged.com/news/el-nino-from-christmas-currents-to-climate-pattern ; NASA Earth Observatory confirms early descriptions in the 1890s via the Lima Geographical Society, without naming Carrillo — https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/features/ElNino/page3.php
- Careful: these are secondary sources only (the Wikipedia/primary pages could not be opened). Say "one of the first written accounts", not "the first".

**16. Gilbert Walker, the British scientist who found the "Southern Oscillation", was trying to forecast India's monsoon after deadly famines.**
- Figure: Columbia IRI says that in 1877 the monsoon failed and India suffered "the worst famine in its history". Walker became Director-General of Observatories in India in 1904. He named the Southern Oscillation in the 1920s: a see-saw of air pressure between the Indian Ocean/Indonesia and the eastern Pacific.
- Source 1: Columbia IRI — https://iridl.ldeo.columbia.edu/maproom/ENSO/New/walker.html
- Source 2: NOAA PSL history (Mock, 1981): Walker named it and built the index in the 1920s–30s — https://psl.noaa.gov/enso/misc/hxsoi.html ; NASA Earth Observatory (as #15).
- Careful: avoid giving one exact year for the name; sources say "1920s". The 1877 famine came before Walker's time; it is the reason the job existed.

**17. In 1969, Jacob Bjerknes showed that the ocean warming (El Niño) and Walker's pressure see-saw are one system.** Scientists now call it ENSO, the El Niño–Southern Oscillation.
- Figure: Bjerknes, J. (1969), "Atmospheric Teleconnections from the Equatorial Pacific", *Monthly Weather Review* 97(3). The paper links the warmings to weaker trade winds and reduced upwelling, "closely tied to the 'Southern Oscillation' of Sir Gilbert Walker".
- Source 1: paper PDF — https://courses.seas.harvard.edu/climate/eli/Courses/EPS281r/Sources/ENSO/Bjerknes-1969.pdf
- Source 2: PBS NOVA timeline — https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/elnino/reach/1969.html
- Careful: Bjerknes was Norwegian-born and worked at UCLA.

**18. El Niño comes back irregularly, every 2 to 7 years, and usually lasts 9–12 months.**
- Source 1: NOAA, "Understanding El Niño" — https://www.noaa.gov/understanding-el-nino
- Source 2: Britannica (as #14), "irregularly at intervals of two to seven years"; Euronews (as #10) says sometimes up to 18 months.
- Careful: it is not a clock. Say "roughly every two to seven years".

**19. El Niño is ancient. Fossil corals show it swinging between weak and strong for thousands of years before industry.**
- Figure: Cobb et al. 2013, *Science* 339:67. Nearly 7,000 years of fossil-coral records. Some of the biggest swings in El Niño strength came in the 17th century, before widespread fossil fuel use.
- Source 1: Scripps news, 9 Jan 2013 — https://scripps.ucsd.edu/news/coral-record-puts-present-day-el-nino-trends-context
- Source 2: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (evidence "in records millions of years old") — https://www.noaa.gov/understanding-el-nino ; IPCC AR6 as summarised by climate.gov (ENSO shows "all sorts of different patterns and amplitudes" through the Holocene) — https://content-drupal.climate.gov/comment/4482
- Careful: "at least 7,000 years" is the safe coral figure. Avoid "millions of years" unless you credit NOAA directly.

## C. MECHANISM

**20. Normally the trade winds blow east to west and pile warm water up in the west, so the sea is about half a metre higher in Indonesia than in Ecuador.**
- Figure: about 0.5 m (1.5 ft).
- Source 1: NOAA PMEL, "What is El Niño?" — https://pmel.noaa.gov/elnino/node/2
- Source 2: Kessler (Univ. of Washington / NOAA PMEL) FAQ: about 0.5 m higher at Indonesia than at Peru — https://faculty.washington.edu/kessler/occasionally-asked-questions.html
- Careful: use "about half a metre" or "about 50 cm". "60 cm" was **not** found in these sources.

**21. The water off Asia is about 8 °C warmer than in the eastern Pacific.**
- Figure: ~8 °C (14 °F) (PMEL). Kessler gives typical values of ~29 °C in the west vs ~23 °C in the east.
- Source 1: NOAA PMEL (as #20). Source 2: Kessler FAQ (as #20).
- Careful: say "around 6–8 degrees", or quote PMEL's 8.

**22. The warm layer is thick in the west and thin in the east, so off South America cold, nutrient-rich water wells up close to the surface.**
- Figure: warm layer about 150 m thick in the west; thermocline about 30 m deep in the east.
- Source 1: Kessler FAQ (as #20). Source 2: NOAA PMEL (upwelling of cold water in the east Pacific) (as #20).

**23. In El Niño the trade winds weaken, warm water sloshes east, and the rain clouds follow it.**
- Quote: "Rainfall follows the warm water eastward, with associated flooding in Peru and drought in Indonesia and Australia." (PMEL)
- Source 1: NOAA PMEL (as #20). Source 2: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (weaker trade winds let warm water slosh east; rainfall shifts east).

**24. The warm water travels as huge, slow underwater waves called Kelvin waves, about 5–10 mph, taking 2–3 months to cross the Pacific.**
- Figure: 5–10 mph (~8–16 km/h); about 2–3 months to cross; sea surface rises about 6–12 inches as they pass.
- Source 1: NOAA Ocean Service, "What are Kelvin waves?" — https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/kelvin-waves.html
- Source 2: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (Kelvin waves form in the west and travel east, often in series).
- Careful: Kessler's FAQ says equatorial waves in general take "3 months to more than a year". Use "two to three months" and credit NOAA.

**25. The feedback loop (the Bjerknes feedback): weaker winds lead to a warmer east, which leads to even weaker winds.**
- Source 1: NOAA climate.gov ENSO blog, June 2023 ("critical feedback mechanism") — https://www.climate.gov/news-features/blogs/june-2023-enso-update-el-ni%C3%B1o-here
- Source 2: Bjerknes 1969 (as #17).
- Careful: the June 2023 blog describes the loop but does not use the name "Bjerknes feedback". The name is standard textbook usage.

**26. How big is the stage? From Indonesia to Ecuador the equatorial Pacific spans roughly half the planet.**
- Figure (DERIVED): Indonesia (~100°E) to Ecuador (~80°W) is ~180° of longitude ≈ half of Earth's ~40,075 km equator, i.e. about 20,000 km. NASA's Earth fact sheet gives an equatorial radius of 6,378 km, so 2π × 6,378 ≈ 40,075 km. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html
- Source 2: WorldAtlas: the Pacific spans "about 19,000 kilometers, some 12,000 miles, from the coast of Colombia west to the Malay Peninsula" — https://www.worldatlas.com/oceans/the-largest-ocean-on-earth.html
- Careful: say "almost halfway around the world". Avoid one exact km figure unless you credit WorldAtlas.

**27. The Niño 3.4 box: the patch that scientists watch.**
- Figure: 5°N–5°S, 120°W–170°W, in the central-eastern equatorial Pacific.
- Source 1: NOAA CPC ENSO alert readme — https://cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/enso-alert-readme.shtml
- Source 2: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (as #18).
- Careful (DERIVED, for visuals): the box is 10° × 50°, roughly 1,100 km × 5,500 km, about 6 million km². That is a rough calculation; label it "roughly".

**28. The rules for calling an El Niño: Niño 3.4 at least +0.5 °C above normal for five overlapping 3-month seasons, plus an atmospheric response.**
- Source 1: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (as #18). Source 2: CPC RONI info sheet, May 2026 — https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso/roni/pdf/roni-info-may-2026.pdf
- **Strength labels:** weak 0.5–0.9, moderate 1.0–1.4, strong 1.5–1.9 (Columbia writes 1.5–2.0), very strong > 2.0 °C ("super" is informal). Source: Columbia Climate School, 9 Jun 2026 — https://news.climate.columbia.edu/2026/06/09/you-asked-what-exactly-is-a-super-el-nino/ ; WRI ("Super El Niño" means more than 2 °C above average) — https://www.wri.org/insights/super-el-nino-impacts-explained . CPC's November discussion uses "historic" for RONI ≥ +2.5 °C.
- Careful: the ≥1.5 / ≥2.0 categories are a widely used convention. **No official NOAA page defining them was found**: the CPC "strengths" page lists probability thresholds, not definitions. Say "scientists usually call...".

**29. Since 1 Feb 2026, NOAA measures El Niño with a new "relative" index (RONI).** RONI compares the Niño 3.4 patch with the whole tropical ocean, because the whole ocean is warming.
- Source 1: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (RONI used operationally since 1 Feb 2026). Source 2: CPC RONI announcement — https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso/roni/announcement.php ; RONI info sheet (as #28).
- Careful: this is why NOAA's numbers can look smaller than Copernicus's raw anomalies.

**30. La Niña is the opposite: stronger trade winds, a colder eastern Pacific, and in many places the reverse impacts.**
- Source 1: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (ENSO warm phase vs cold phase; transitions take 1–6 months). Source 2: NOAA PMEL (the east Pacific cold tongue can run ~3 °C below normal in La Niña).

## D. WORLD IMPACTS

**31. Typical El Niño fingerprints around the world (Dec–Feb):** wetter coastal Ecuador and northwestern Peru, southern Brazil, central Argentina and equatorial East Africa; drier northern South America, Central America and southern Africa; drought risk in Indonesia, Australia and southern Asia; milder winters across the northern US and western Canada; a wetter US South.
- Source 1: NOAA CPC ENSO cycle page — https://cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/ensocycle/elninosfc.shtml
- Source 2: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (as #18); WMO, 8 Oct 2026, list of regions for this event (as #8). WMO lists drier conditions for "the Indian subcontinent".
- Careful: these are tendencies. Every El Niño differs (see the Australia 2026 counter-example in #13).

**32. El Niño suppresses Atlantic hurricanes and boosts eastern/central Pacific hurricanes.**
- Mechanism: stronger vertical wind shear over the Atlantic tears storms apart; warmer water in the Pacific fuels them.
- Source 1: NOAA AOML — https://www.aoml.noaa.gov/how-does-el-nino-impact-atlantic-hurricane-season/
- Source 2: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (as #18).
- Careful: El Niño "tends to" reduce Atlantic activity. It still allows hurricanes.

**33. El Niño temporarily lifts the planet's average temperature by a tenth or two of a degree.**
- Figure: "A typical El Niño temporarily adds about 0.2C to average global temperature." (Jeff Knight, UK Met Office, 2023). NASA estimated El Niño added 0.12 °C (0.2 °F) to 2016. Climate Central: strong events add "about 0.1°C to 0.3°C".
- Source 1: Met Office blog, 2023 — https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2023/what-can-the-world-expect-from-the-developing-el-nino
- Source 2: NASA/NOAA, Jan 2017 — https://www.nasa.gov/press-release/nasa-noaa-data-show-2016-warmest-year-on-record-globally ; Climate Central, 7 Oct 2026 — https://www.climatecentral.org/climate-matters/el-nino-global-temperatures-explained
- Careful: say "about 0.1 to 0.2 °C", and stress "temporarily".

**34. Record years: 2016 came after the 2015–16 El Niño; 2024 (the current record) came after the 2023–24 El Niño.**
- Figure: 2024 = 15.10 °C, +0.72 °C vs 1991–2020, 0.12 °C warmer than 2023. C3S says ENSO "also contributed", alongside human-caused warming.
- Source 1: C3S Global Climate Highlights 2024 — https://climate.copernicus.eu/copernicus-2024-first-year-exceed-15degc-above-pre-industrial-level
- Source 2: NASA, 2016 record (as #33); Met Office 2023 ("the second year of the event remains the hottest year on record", i.e. 2016 at the time).
- Careful / **NOT VERIFIED**: "1998 was a record year". No primary page was opened (the NOAA 1999 release archive did not load). If used, say "1998, a strong El Niño year, was then among the hottest on record". Britannica confirms 1997–98 had an ONI peak of 2.3 °C.

**35. Peru's anchovy catch collapsed in the 1972–73 El Niño.**
- Figure: from a record 13.1 million tonnes (1970) to 1.7 million tonnes (1973). FAO: El Niño was "a primary cause of recruitment failure", made worse by heavy fishing.
- Source 1: FAO — https://www.fao.org/4/w4248e/w4248e30.htm
- Source 2: NOAA "Fisheries of Peru, 1972-73" (1970 landings 12.3 million tons) — https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/30114 ; UCAR "An El Niño Fish Tale" (nearly 10 million metric tons in 1970; overfishing plus El Niño) — https://scied.ucar.edu/el-nino-fish-tale
- Careful: sources disagree on the 1970 peak (10–13.1 Mt). Say "more than 10 million tonnes" and "it crashed by around 85–90%" (DERIVED from FAO). Always mention overfishing too.

**36. The 1997–98 El Niño, then the strongest of the century: about 23,000 deaths worldwide.**
- Figure: 23,000 deaths "from natural disasters" (World Bank). NOAA (via CBS/AP, 2002): about 24,000 deaths, $34 billion in damage, 6 million people displaced.
- Source 1: World Bank blog, 1 Dec 2015 (Axel van Trotsenburg) — https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/eastasiapacific/we-must-prepare-now-another-major-el-nino
- Source 2: CBS News, 7 Mar 2002, citing NOAA — https://www.cbsnews.com/news/signs-of-el-ninos-rebirth/
- Careful: death tolls are estimates. IFLScience notes there are "no definitive statistics". Best wording: "an estimated 23,000 people died in the disasters linked to it". For damage, say "tens of billions of dollars in direct damage" ($34 bn per NOAA in 2002). The "$32–96 billion" range was **not** verified.

**37. The long-term economic hit is far bigger: trillions of dollars.**
- Figure: Callahan & Mankin, *Science*, 2023. Global income losses of $4.1 trillion (1982–83) and $5.7 trillion (1997–98) in the five years after each event. The 2023 El Niño could cost up to $3 trillion by 2029. Projected 21st-century losses: $84 trillion.
- Source 1: Dartmouth news, 18 May 2023 — https://home.dartmouth.edu/news/2023/05/years-after-el-nino-global-economy-loses-trillions

**38. 2015–16 helped drive the longest and most widespread coral bleaching ever recorded at the time (2014–2017).**
- Figure: more than 70% of the world's coral reefs had bleaching-level heat stress (NOAA Coral Reef Watch). Eakin et al. via climate.gov: more than 75% of tropical reefs, with mortality-level stress at nearly 30%. NOAA calls it the Third Global Coral Bleaching Event (June 2014–May 2017).
- Source 1: NOAA Coral Reef Watch — https://coralreefwatch.noaa.gov/satellite/analyses_guidance/global_coral_bleaching_2014-17_status.php
- Source 2: climate.gov (Eakin et al. 2018, BAMS) — https://climate.gov/print/832233
- Careful: the event began **before** El Niño arrived; El Niño "spread and worsened" it. Don't say El Niño caused it alone.

**39. The Panama Canal had to cut ship traffic in 2023, and El Niño was a key reason.**
- Figure: 2023 rainfall was ~30% below average. World Weather Attribution found El Niño cut rainfall by ~8% and made such a dry year unlikely without it. The canal cut transits from a normal ~36 a day to 25 a day (Nov 2023). Panama had its third-driest year in 143 years.

## E. MYTHS

**42. Myth: "El Niño is caused by climate change."** It isn't. It is a natural ocean–atmosphere cycle, recorded by Peruvian fishermen centuries ago and visible in at least 7,000 years of coral records (#19). The IPCC says it is "virtually certain" that ENSO will keep existing, and has **low confidence** in how its sea-temperature swings will change.
- Source 1: climate.gov ENSO blog on IPCC AR6 (as #19). Source 2: Science Feedback, 8 Jul 2026 — https://science.feedback.org/el-nino-misinformation/
- **But (careful wording):**
  - Cai et al. 2014 (*Nature Climate Change* 4:111) projected that extreme El Niños could double, from about once every 20 years to once every 10, under greenhouse warming. NOAA PMEL, 3 Feb 2014 — https://www.pmel.noaa.gov/news-and-media/highlights/frequency-extreme-el-ninos-double-21st-century . Note: their "extreme" is defined by rainfall.
  - Cai et al. 2023 (*Nature Climate Change*): human emissions mean strong El Niño and La Niña events have occurred more often since 1960. CSIRO — https://www.csiro.au/en/news/all/articles/2023/may/climate-change-affecting-el-nino
  - Cole et al., *Science*, 27 Aug 2026: Galápagos corals suggest eastern-Pacific El Niños of the last ~40 years are the strongest in a millennium (~40% stronger than pre-industrial, per Live Science). Live Science — https://livescience.com/planet-earth/climate-change/this-is-such-a-clear-story-climate-change-is-supercharging-el-nino-to-unprecedented-strengths-study-finds ; second: Julia Cole in The Conversation via WLRN, 31 Aug 2026 — https://www.wlrn.org/environment/2026-08-31/galapagos-corals-show-el-nino-events-are-more-extreme-now-than-in-past-1-000-years
  - Suggested line: "Climate change didn't invent El Niño, but there is growing evidence it may be making the strong ones stronger. Scientists are still debating how much." The "40%" figure is from Live Science's summary; Cole's own article gives no percentage.

**43. Myth: "El Niño makes everywhere hotter."** No. It reshuffles heat and rain. The southern US tends to be cooler and wetter in an El Niño winter (NOAA). Some places get floods while others get drought. Australia in 2026 has been unusually wet (#13).
- Source 1: NOAA "Understanding El Niño" (southern US "wetter and cooler") — https://www.noaa.gov/understanding-el-nino
- Source 2: NOAA 2026–27 winter outlook (near-normal temperatures favoured in parts of the Southeast, including Florida) (as #12).

**44. Myth: "El Niño is a storm."** It is not a storm. It is a pattern of ocean temperatures and winds across the tropical Pacific that lasts months and shifts where storms form. NOAA calls it "one of the most influential climate drivers on Earth".
- Source 1: NOAA "Understanding El Niño". Source 2: NOAA PMEL definition (as #20).
